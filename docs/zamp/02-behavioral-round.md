## A) What is being screened

R2 tested whether you can do the job. **This round is not a competence check** — competence
is the price of entry to a CEO's calendar, not the thing being measured. He is calibrating
you against a bar, not against a job description.

Zamp publishes 13 values. For a lead backend hire they collapse into five questions:

| Screen | Zamp values behind it | What kills you |
|---|---|---|
| Will they own the outcome, not the ticket? | Act like an owner · Take initiative over and above · Deliver outstanding results | Stories where someone else set the goal and you executed |
| Will they move at startup speed? | Velocity is survival · Imagine the future and make it reality | "We spent a quarter aligning stakeholders" told as a badge of honour |
| Will they say the hard thing? | Courage over comfort · Radical transparency | Only conflict stories where you deferred, or only ones where you won |
| Do they simplify or accrete? | Break it down, solve simply · Build with all your heart | Architecture stories that add a component per problem |
| Will they still be here at intensity in 18 months? | Unwavering intensity · Relentless curiosity | An unexplained gap, or a "why leaving" that sounds like fatigue |

**Your profile carries two specific risks.** Everything below is built to defuse them:

1. **Big-co → 60-person startup.** Four and a half years at Stripe: review chains, PMs,
   staff engineers, staging environments. The worry is that you need that scaffolding.
   Antidote: every story shows you deciding, not aligning — and [§D](#d-the-career-arc--the-spine-of-the-hour)
   has the fact that settles it outright.
2. **The career break.** At a company whose value is literally *Unwavering intensity*, a
   voluntary gap with no offer in hand invites "did he burn out, and will he again?"
   Antidote: short, unapologetic, forward-facing, with evidence of output. This repo is that evidence.

## B) What "spikes" means

Founder shorthand, out of the Palantir / Keith Rabois school of hiring: **hire spiky people —
exceptional at one thing — over well-rounded people with no weaknesses.** The reasoning is
structural, not stylistic:

- At a large company the system carries you, so it hires to eliminate weaknesses. "Solid
  everywhere" is a safe bet because process catches the rest.
- At 60 people **you carry the system**, and one person's peak becomes a company capability.
  There is no process to catch anything. Averages don't compound; peaks do.

So the question behind the whole hour is: **is this person top 1% at something, and is that
something useful to us?**

### The test — four properties

A spike has all four. "Improved latency 10%" has none, which is why HR used it as the
anti-example.

| | Property | The 10% latency example fails because… |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Step change, not increment.** An order of magnitude, or a category change: batch → incremental, manual → automated, didn't exist → exists. | 10% is a tuning result. It is on the same curve. |
| 2 | **Counterfactual — it does not happen without you.** You saw it, or you were the only one who would take it on. | Someone else would have hit 10% next quarter. |
| 3 | **Business consequence** — money, customers, time, risk. Not a system metric. | Latency is a system metric. Nobody upstairs felt it. |
| 4 | **Comparative** — unusual for someone at your level, at that time. | It is exactly what the level is expected to produce. |

**Property 3 is where you will lose points.** Every number in your prep is a system metric:
runtime, freshness, p99. **Translate each one up a level** — to dollars, to customers, to a
deadline that was going to be missed, to a risk that got removed. A CEO does not care that a
dashboard got faster; he cares what became possible.

### Three traps

- **Don't tell three medium stories.** The instinct under a broad question is to demonstrate
  range. Range is the opposite of a spike, and a founder remembers exactly one thing about
  you. Pick your peak and go deep.
- **Don't under-claim.** "The team delivered", "I helped with", "it kind of fell to me" — all
  of it reads as *not having a spike*. If you were the reason something happened, say so
  plainly. That is accuracy, not arrogance.
- **Don't claim to be strong everywhere.** Spiky people have flat spots and founders know it.
  Claiming none is the well-rounded answer, and it fails this screen. Have **one honest
  weakness** ready, named specifically, with what you do about it — not a disguised strength.

### Spikes are not only work

Founders read these as raw ability and self-direction: GATE **454 out of 108,495** — top 0.4%
of a hundred thousand people — and IIIT-Hyderabad. Don't lead with an eight-year-old
credential, but it is one line, externally verifiable, and for this audience it lands
instantly. Same for what you did with the break.

## C) Framework and delivery

**Decode → Select → Deliver.**

- **Decode** — answer the question behind the question. Every section below opens with it.
- **Select** — pick on *scope first*, then relevance, uniqueness, recency. Scope means blast
  radius: how many people, how much money, how permanent the decision. A tidy story about a
  small thing loses to a messier story about a big thing.
- **Deliver** — **CARL**: Context, Actions, Results, Learnings. A refinement of STAR; the *L*
  is what most candidates drop and where senior signal lives.

**Delivery rules — these matter more than the content:**

- **Claim first, evidence second.** Founders context-switch fast and decide early. Bury the
  lede and the answer is over before the payoff.
- **20 seconds of context, hard cap.** Your instinct will be to explain Iceberg and Pinot.
  Don't. Say "our analytical store" and let him ask. **Cut most technology names** — they mean
  nothing to him, and spending words on them signals you can't tell what matters.
- **"I", not "we".** Your drafts are full of "we decided", "we compared". He cannot grade a
  "we". Say what *you* did; credit others explicitly and separately — that reads as generous,
  where "we" reads as hiding.
- **Numbers, in money, customers, time or risk.** 12h → 3h. $600/day. 5,000 merchants. These
  are your best asset and you underuse them — but translate each one up a level (§B).
- **Land the learning as a rule you now apply.** "I learned to communicate better" is
  worthless. "I now agree the evaluation criteria before anyone builds a POC, because
  otherwise the POC becomes about whose idea it was" is a rule.
- **Two minutes, then stop.** He will take the conversation where he wants it. Silence after a
  complete answer is his job to fill, not yours.
- **Disagree with him at least once**, if you actually do. *Courage over comfort* is one of the
  thirteen values and this round is a live test of it. Agreeing with everything is a worse
  outcome than being wrong about something.

**Phrases from your notes that must not be said out loud:**

> "no growth and recognition" · "constantly understaffed" · "bureaucracy" ·
> "potential was not fully utilised" · "recharge" · "burnt out"

Every one of them makes the problem external and makes you the passenger. They also
contradict your own résumé — you were *promoted to Lead in March 2025*. Use the promotion; it
proves the exit wasn't about how things were going for you.

**Manager churn, reorgs, losing the senior technical voice, and unfunded tech debt are true
and sayable** — but
only as evidence for a single claim, and only when followed by what you did about it
([§G](#g-why-are-you-leaving-stripe)). Said as a list of things that were done to you, they
join the list above. The test: could a listener summarise your reason in one sentence? If not,
you are venting.

## D) The career arc — the spine of the hour

The second half of the brief: a **conversation around the journey**. So this is not "have one
great story ready." It is a walk from IIIT-H to now, and while you walk it he is watching for
the **inflection points — where the line jumps rather than continues.**

What he is reading off the arc:

- **Did scope grow faster than tenure?** Scope-above-level is the cleanest career spike there is.
- **Were the moves chosen or accepted?** Agency. Did you steer, or did things happen to you?
- **Does each step build on the last**, or is it a random walk?
- **Where does the line flatten, and do you know why?**

| Period | The step | Inflection |
|---|---|---|
| IIIT-H, M.Tech | GATE **454 / 108,495** | **Yes** — top 0.4% of a hundred thousand |
| Tesco, 2018–19 | Redesigned the GHS Orders queueing system | Baseline — component ownership |
| Flipkart, 2019–21 | **DRI of DCC** — proactively detect accounting discrepancies, categorise to the most granular level, auto-fix where possible | **Yes** — platform DRI as an SWE I/II, scope well above level. And a *category* change: manual discrepancy chasing → automatic detection and repair |
| Recko, 2021 | Left a 100,000-person company for a startup | **Yes** — the agency move |
| Stripe, via acquisition | Took Recko's reconciliation product into Stripe: Alpha → Private Beta → Public Beta → 5,000+ merchants | **Yes** — most acquired products quietly die in integration |
| Stripe, Accounting Foundations | Re-architected revenue recognition reporting; IC2 → Lead, Mar 2025 | **Yes** — the technical peak ([§F](#f-the-spike-question)) |
| The last year | Three managers in two years, reorgs, the staff engineer with the platform's full history leaving, team bandwidth to the UBB code yellow — nothing multi-quarter survives | **Flat.** He will see it. Name it first — [§G](#g-why-are-you-leaving-stripe) |

Three things to pull out of that table and say deliberately:

**1. The Recko move is the most underused fact you own.** Every CEO interviewing a Stripe
engineer is silently asking *can he function in a company with no scaffolding?* You have
already answered it — you left a 100,000-person company for a ~20-person startup, on purpose,
and it turned into Stripe. It is currently nowhere in your prep. Say it out loud: **"I've made
this move before. I left Flipkart for a small startup, and that's how I got to Stripe."** That
one sentence does more for your startup-fit than anything else you can say.

**2. The arc is one domain, and it is Zamp's domain.** Flipkart accounting discrepancies →
Recko reconciliation → Stripe revenue recognition. Eight years pointed at financial
correctness. Present it as a deliberate line, not a chronology — "I've optimised for staying
in this problem space" is a much stronger sentence.

**3. DCC is the closest thing on your résumé to Zamp's product.** Detect discrepancies,
categorise them granularly, fix automatically where possible — that is Zamp's pitch, and you
built a version of it in 2019 without the models. Say that to the person who has to sell it.

**The flat spot.** Seven years of rising line, then a flat year. Do not let him find it — name
it yourself as you get there. Arriving at your own weak point before the interviewer does is a
*Radical transparency* signal, and it removes the only gotcha available in the conversation.

### "Tell me about yourself" / "Walk me through your career"

Not a warm-up — it is the spine, and he will branch off it. Four beats: *what you do → the one
thing you're known for → the arc → why you're in this room.* 60–75 seconds.

> "I'm a backend and data engineer, eight years in, and almost all of it has been financial
> systems where being fast is worth nothing if the numbers are wrong. Flipkart accounting
> first — I was the DRI for a platform that detected accounting discrepancies and fixed them
> automatically, which is not far from what you're building. Then I left for Recko, a small
> reconciliation startup, and came into Stripe through that acquisition.
>
> The work I'm best known for is Stripe's revenue recognition reporting platform. I was the
> DRI, and I took it from whole-world batch — a 12-hour job — to incremental processing at
> three hours, then to sub-hour, with dashboards from ten-to-twenty seconds down to
> sub-second, and about $600 a day off the infrastructure bill. Before that I led the
> reconciliation product after the acquisition, out to 5,000 merchants.
>
> I was promoted to Lead last March. Where I got stuck is that planning ran top-down and the
> platform work I could see needed doing kept not making the quarter — so I took a planned
> break, handled some family things, and spent it on fundamentals.
>
> So: eight years in one problem space, and I've made the big-company-to-startup move once
> before. That's why Zamp specifically rather than generally."

## F) The spike question

*"What's the most impressive thing you've done?" · "What are you exceptionally good at?" ·
"Why you over someone with the same résumé?"* — all the same question. Answer it with S1, once,
and don't hedge into three options.

**Decode.** *Is there a peak here at all, and is it high enough that it would raise our bar
rather than sit inside it?*

> "The one I'd point to is Stripe's revenue recognition reporting platform.
>
> There was a hard requirement to get reports under three hours — [what it unblocked]. The
> pipeline ran twelve hours as a whole-world batch, and the work in flight was tuning it:
> more executors, better partitioning. My read was that tuning doesn't close a 4x gap, and
> that the model itself was the bug — in a system where the overwhelming majority of the
> world hasn't changed since the last run, recomputing all of it every time is the problem.
>
> So I proposed decomposing it into incremental, change-based processing. The hard part isn't
> incremental processing; it's incremental processing on a financial system, where a wrong
> number isn't a bug, it's a restatement. I was the DRI and took it from architecture to
> production.
>
> Twelve hours to three, and we hit the milestone. Then I pushed it further — incremental
> materialisation instead of full table rebuilds, and a real serving layer — which got
> freshness under an hour and dashboards from ten-to-twenty seconds to sub-second. About $600
> a day off the infrastructure bill, so a bit over $200k a year.
>
> The part I'd actually claim is the first move. Nobody asked for a re-architecture. The ask
> was to make the batch job faster."

That closing line is the spike — it supplies property 2, the counterfactual, and without it
this is just a good optimisation story. **Verify it is true before you say it**: were you the
one who proposed the model change, and was the team otherwise tuning? If it was handed to you
as a re-architecture, cut the line and lead with the correctness constraint instead.

**Also find, before the round:** what the sub-3-hour requirement actually unblocked. Who was
blocked, which close or customer commitment depended on it. That is property 3, it is missing,
and it is the difference between "engineer made pipeline faster" and "engineer unblocked the
business."

## G) "Why are you leaving Stripe?"

**Decode.** Three questions in one: *(a) Is there something you're running from that will
follow you here? (b) Is the thing you want the thing we actually have, or will you leave us in
18 months for the same reason? (c) Do you own your part, or is everything done to you?*

**Shape: 70% pull, 30% push.** Push-first answers sound like grievance no matter how carefully
worded.

> "I was at Stripe for four and a half years, and it was a good run — I was the DRI for the
> revenue recognition reporting rebuild, took it from a 12-hour batch pipeline to under three
> and then to sub-hour, and I was promoted to Lead in March 2025.
>
> What changed is that nothing could accumulate. Three managers in two years plus a couple of
> reorgs, so nobody was in the seat long enough to own a multi-quarter bet — and then the
> staff engineer who'd been on the platform since the beginning left in 2025. He had the full
> history and the standing to actually shape technical direction; once he was gone there was
> nobody at the table making the technical case, and priorities just came down. Around the
> same time the company called a code yellow on usage-based billing and most of the team's
> bandwidth went there — which was the right call, and I'd have made the same one in their
> seat. The net of it was that the work we knew was hurting us, the tech debt and the
> reliability gaps generating our incidents, never made the cut.
>
> I didn't just sit with that. The biggest reliability problem we had, I went and worked on
> anyway — pulled the incident history, categorised it, found the systemic cause, and built
> the staging fidelity and business invariant checking to catch that class of bug before
> production. Nobody asked me to. It was never going to arrive through planning.
>
> There's a limit to how long you do that in the margins, though. What I want is to be
> somewhere a decision made in January still means something in June — where the engineer
> with the context is in the room when technical direction gets set rather than arguing for a
> seat, and where the distance from spotting a problem to shipping the fix is days."

Then stop. 80 seconds, and that is the ceiling — see risk 3.

**"Nothing could accumulate" is the thesis, and everything else is evidence for it.** That is
the whole trick with this answer. Manager churn, the reorgs, the staff engineer's departure
and the code yellow are not four complaints; they are four causes of one effect, and the
effect is a real, structural, founder-legible problem — institutional memory kept walking out
of the door, so nothing multi-quarter survived. Told that way it is an observation about how
large organisations fail. Told as a list, it is a man with a lot of grievances.

**The staff engineer is the best beat in the answer — don't skip it.** It is the only part
with no complaint in it at all: you lost a colleague you valued, and the thing you lost was
*someone who could make the technical case*. It carries no villain, it explains the flat year
mechanically, and it sets the closing ask up perfectly — because what you're describing
wanting is to *be* that person, somewhere small enough that context and influence sit in the
same seat by default.

**Why this works:** the promotion pre-empts "was he managed out"; the specifics make it
checkable rather than a vague grievance about priorities; every complaint is about *structure*,
not about any person; and — the load-bearing part — you don't leave it as a complaint, because
paragraph three is you doing the work anyway. That converts "I had no say" from a grievance
into evidence of *Act like an owner*. The closing ask is concrete, and it is a request a
founder can actually grant, since he runs the planning process at his own company.

### Three risks in this answer, and the fixes

**1. Never sound like you resented the code yellow.** This is the bigger one. A founder hears
"code yellow" and thinks *that was the company's top priority — a good engineer goes and
helps.* If any part of the delivery reads as annoyance at being pulled onto the company's
most urgent revenue work, you have failed *Act like an owner* and "this is your company" in a
single sentence. Hence **"that was the right call and I'd have made the same one"** — say it
early and mean it. The grievance is never the code yellow itself; it is that the shape held
long enough to become the steady state, and that the platform work was structurally never
going to get funded once the pattern set. Be precise on the facts here: how long it ran, and
whether you worked on UBB yourself or were left holding the platform while the team went.

**2. Never say "I didn't have a say" as a bare statement.** At Lead level an interviewer can
hear *he couldn't influence his own roadmap* — a verdict on you, not on Stripe. Always pair it
with what you did regardless. The sentence is "planning was top-down, so I went and did it
anyway," never "so it didn't get done."

**3. Grievance density — the one that will actually sink this.** You have five true things:
manager churn, reorgs, the staff engineer leaving, the code yellow, and unfunded tech debt.
One reason, well told, reads as clarity. Five reasons read as someone who accumulates
grievances and will accumulate them here too. The fix is the thesis line — they are *causes of
one effect*, and you say the effect first ("nothing could accumulate") so every fact that
follows is evidence rather than another item on a list. **Do not extend this answer.** If he
wants more he will ask, and the follow-up table is where the rest lives.

Related discipline: never deliver any of it with heat. The same words at 80% warmth read as
diagnosis; at 100% they read as venting.

### The follow-up this invites: "you were the Lead — why weren't you that person?"

Expect it, because the dates line up: you were promoted in March 2025 and he left the same
year. Handled badly this is the sharpest question in the answer — it turns your best beat into
*he needed someone senior to advocate for him*. Handled well it is the most self-aware thing
you say all hour.

> "Fair question, and partly I did try — the reliability work I just described is exactly me
> doing it without a mandate. But I was a few months into Lead when he left, and he had years
> of standing in that org. At that size, influence over technical direction isn't something a
> title gives you; it's relationships across a lot of teams, and it takes a couple of years to
> build — and I'd have been rebuilding them straight into the next reorg. That's honestly a
> big part of what I want next: somewhere small enough that the person with the context is in
> the room by default, instead of spending two years earning the right to be there."

It owns a real limitation without self-flagellation, it is obviously true at Stripe's scale,
and it converts into a want that a 60-person company uniquely satisfies.

**On discretion:** usage-based billing is a shipped, public Stripe product, so naming it is
fine. Keep it at the level of "the company prioritised UBB hard" and stay off unreleased
roadmap, internal numbers — a founder reads volunteering that as a preview of how you'd
talk about *his* company later. The staff engineer needs no name and no story beyond "he left";
speaking well of a former colleague costs nothing and is quietly noticed.

| They ask | You say |
|---|---|
| "Three managers in two years — was that the real problem?" | Deflect the fragility read immediately. "The churn itself I can work with — I've onboarded managers before. What I couldn't work around was that each change reset the priorities, so anything longer than a quarter never survived a handover." |
| "You were the Lead — why weren't you that person?" | The sharpest follow-up here. Scripted above. |
| "Did you work on the code yellow yourself?" | If yes, say so plainly and briefly — going where the company needs you is the answer a founder wants, and it costs you nothing here. If you were left maintaining the platform instead, say that: it explains the flat year better than anything else you can offer. |
| "So you didn't want to work on the top priority?" | The trap. "No — I worked on it, and I'd have prioritised it the same way. What I couldn't get comfortable with was a year of that being the steady state, with no route for the platform work even after the escalation ended." |
| "Did you try to change it?" | The most important follow-up — you need a crisp yes. S3 is the proof you built it anyway; add what you raised in planning and in 1:1s, and what happened. |
| "Why couldn't you influence the roadmap?" | No defensiveness. "I could influence what my team did. What I couldn't influence was the shape of the quarter — that was set above the team, and the further you sit from that, the more you're absorbing decisions rather than making them." Then pivot to what you want instead. |
| "What would have kept you?" | Concrete, not "nothing": a seat in planning and a zero-to-one surface with a small team on it. "Nothing" reads as bitterness. |
| "Was your manager aware?" | Yes — raised in 1:1s, on priorities and timelines. Don't turn this into a story about the manager. |
| "Stripe pays well — what about comp?" | Don't pretend it's irrelevant. "Comp matters, and I'd expect the equity to reflect the stage. It isn't the reason I left." |

## H) "Why did you leave without another offer lined up?"

**Decode.** Pure risk assessment: *were you actually let go? is the gap longer than it looks?
are you desperate — will you take anything and leave when something better appears? and have
eight months off blunted you?*

**Length signals guilt.** 30 seconds, flat, then pivot to evidence. Don't add a fourth reason.
Don't over-explain "personal commitments" — one clause, and if he probes, "family matters, all
settled," and move on.

> "It was a deliberate choice. I'd worked continuously for eight years, I had some family
> things to take care of, and I didn't want to run a job search on evenings and half-attention
> — I've seen people take the wrong job that way. So I took a defined break, handled what I
> needed to, and spent the bulk of it on fundamentals: distributed systems, system design,
> DSA. I've written up around sixty designs in that time as a study set. I've been
> interviewing since [month] and I'm being deliberate about fit rather than speed."

A founder is likelier to *respect* this than an HM is — walking away from Stripe comp without
a safety net is an agency signal, and it rhymes with the Recko move. Let the deliberateness
show. But never "recharge" or "burnt out": at a company that lists *Unwavering intensity* as a
value those words do specific damage. "Deliberate", "defined", "planned" is the register.

| They ask | You say |
|---|---|
| "How long has it been?" | The plain number, no wince. Follow immediately with what you built during it. |
| "What did you learn in the break?" | The best question you'll get — a *Relentless curiosity* gift. Name one thing concretely: durable execution / Temporal semantics, or the Pinot segment-replace job, and what it changed in how you'd design. |
| "Would you go back to Stripe?" | "No — same reasons I left." Hesitation here is expensive. |
| "Are you talking to other companies?" | Honest and brief. Don't inflate; inflated pipelines get checked. |

## I) "Tell me about a time you faced conflict"

**Decode.** *Can you disagree with someone and still have a working relationship afterwards?
Do you have a mechanism, or do you rely on rank, volume, or giving in? And is the conflict
real* — a story with no genuine tension reads as evasion.

Lead with **S4 (Pinot vs. Iceberg)**: technical, peer-level, hard numbers, and it resolved into
a synthesis rather than a winner. Switch to **S5 (the PM)** for a non-engineer or a
prioritisation question. Hold **S6** for "a time you were wrong."

**For a CEO, compress the mechanism by half.** He doesn't want your criteria matrix; he wants
to know whether you have spine and whether you were right.

### S4 — the delivery (~2 min)

**Context (20s).** "We were designing the next generation of the reporting platform. A
colleague had done the POC for our analytical store — both ingestion modes — and wanted
real-time as the primary path because it gave us near-live reporting. I disagreed, and it was
awkward: he'd spent weeks on that POC, so arguing against real-time landed as arguing against
his work."

**Actions**, in this order:

1. **Named the real disagreement.** "We weren't disagreeing about whether his POC worked — it
   worked. We were disagreeing about what to optimise for. He was optimising freshness; I was
   optimising recoverability. Once I said that out loud the temperature dropped."
2. **Made it concrete.** Pulled actual past incidents — bad events in the pipeline, a schema
   change, a merchant needing a backfill — and asked what each approach does in that specific
   case, instead of trading opinions.
3. **Agreed the criteria before the answer.** Freshness, recovery, backfill, schema evolution,
   query latency, cost, operational load — written down and agreed *first*, so the comparison
   wasn't about whose idea it was.
4. **Escalated the trade-off, not the fight.** Real-time meant accepting a recovery posture
   with business consequences in a financial product, so the PM made that call explicitly
   rather than two engineers making it implicitly.

**Results.** "We didn't pick one. Iceberg became the durable, replayable source of truth with
incremental materialisation; Pinot became the serving layer, loaded through controlled
materialisation rather than being the system of record. He got the freshness — three hours to
under one — and I got recoverability. Dashboards went from 10–20 seconds to sub-second. And he
stayed on as the owner of the serving layer."

**Learnings.** "The rule I took from it: agree the evaluation criteria before anyone builds the
POC. We had the POC first, so the discussion started already attached to a person. And
technical disagreements are usually priority disagreements in a technical costume — get both
priorities named and most of them dissolve."

**Deliberately not said:** "real-time doesn't work for recoverability" — too absolute, and
anyone who knows Pinot will take it apart. Say "for our workload and our recovery requirements."

| They ask | You say |
|---|---|
| "What if he still disagreed?" | "Run the failure scenarios in a production-like environment and let that decide. And since it carried a business trade-off, the PM makes the call — not the louder engineer." |
| "How did he take it?" | Concrete and generous: he owned the serving layer; his POC is why you knew the freshness was reachable at all. |
| "Were you right?" | "Partly. He was right that the freshness was reachable — my objection wasn't whether it worked, it was what happens when it breaks." Never claim a clean win. |
| "Ever lost one of these?" | S6. Have it ready — this follow-up is common. |

### S5 — the stakeholder version (backup, ~90s)

**C:** mid-dashboard-redesign, the PM wanted a full Accountant role experience — new
navigation, Overview changes — on top of a redesign already in flight. **A:** you argued for
sequencing, not for doing less: build the role and the capability, keep the UI minimal, put it
in front of merchants, and commit to the full experience at a defined adoption threshold (~20
merchants). The PM held firm; rather than grind, you brought your manager in and reframed the
question from *yes/no* to *in what order*. **R:** phase 1 shipped without blocking the
redesign; the PM had a written commitment and a trigger rather than a refusal. **L:** "When
you've made your case twice and the other person is still firm, more debate is just cost. Get
the decision-maker the trade-off in writing and convert a yes/no into a sequence."

Two cautions: (i) frame the pushback as *sequencing and evidence*, never as scope reduction,
or it reads as an engineer avoiding work; (ii) frame the escalation as "routed the decision to
its owner", never "asked my manager to settle it." **And know how it ended** — if adoption
never cleared the threshold, that is the *better* ending, because the phasing saved the build.
Don't tell this story without the ending.

## J) "What problem did you identify?"

**Decode.** *Do you find work nobody assigned you — and do you finish it? At what altitude do
you see problems: the bug, or the pattern that produces the bugs?* The purest **Take initiative
over and above** question, and the best fit for this round because it is self-initiated by
construction.

**Story: S3.** Two fixes to your material. **One spine, not four initiatives** — your notes
list staging, invariant checkers, onboarding and documentation as peers, which sounds like a
backlog; tell it as *incidents → one insight → one intervention → result*. And **numbers**,
which are entirely missing ([§N](#n-gaps-to-close-before-the-round)).

> "We were taking a steady stream of production incidents and each one was being handled as its
> own fire. Nobody had asked me to look at it in aggregate, but I pulled the incident history —
> [N] incidents over [period] — and categorised them by root cause rather than by symptom.
>
> The pattern was clear: most of them weren't infrastructure failures. They were correctness
> problems — a merchant configured in a way we didn't handle, an input variant we'd never seen,
> a business invariant quietly violated — and we found out in production, because staging
> didn't look enough like production to catch them and our monitoring was technical, not
> business-level. Green dashboards, wrong numbers.
>
> So I built two things. A staging environment with real fidelity — it could run against
> production upstreams with the staging pipeline, or fully against staging dependencies for
> isolated end-to-end runs — seeded with real merchant data and the input variants that had
> historically caused incidents. And a set of business invariant checkers running against the
> staging output, so every incident class we'd seen became a permanent check rather than tribal
> knowledge.
>
> [Results: bugs caught pre-production, incident reduction, teams adopting it.]
>
> What I'd do differently: I should have done the categorisation six months earlier. We had the
> data the whole time; nobody had spent the afternoon on it. Now, when I see repeat incidents,
> the first thing I check is whether anyone has aggregated them."

**Say the ownership beat explicitly** — "nobody asked me to" is the phrase that makes this an
initiative story instead of a project story.

| They ask | You say |
|---|---|
| "How did you get the time?" | The honest answer. If it was alongside roadmap work, say so — that *is* the signal. |
| "How did you convince other teams?" | Whatever actually worked: the categorisation itself was the argument, or you made it zero-effort to adopt. |
| "What's an invariant you checked?" | Have two real ones ready, in finance terms — recognised revenue over a period reconciling to the ledger, a report's totals tying back to source events. This is Zamp's exact product problem; a crisp answer is worth a lot. |
| "Did it actually reduce incidents?" | If you don't know, say what you do know. Don't invent a percentage. |

## K) "What are you looking for in your next job?" / "Why Zamp?"

**Decode.** The mirror of §G — *does what he wants exist here, and is it specific enough to be
checkable?* Vague wants ("growth", "impact", "ownership") are unfalsifiable and get discounted
entirely. Three concrete criteria, each traceable to your history and verifiable about Zamp.

**The thing you are burying: you have already built his product.** Reconciliation at
Recko/Stripe — 5,000 merchants, configurable matching, opt-in/opt-out, date-based rules — is
the first item on Zamp's product surface. Revenue recognition and close are the next two.
Almost no candidate walks in having shipped the exact thing. To the person who has to *sell*
reconciliation, that is the most interesting sentence you own.

> "Three things, and Zamp is the first place where all three line up.
>
> First, the domain. I've spent five years on exactly this problem — I built Stripe's
> reconciliation product after the Recko acquisition, through Alpha and Beta to 5,000
> merchants, and before that the revenue recognition reporting platform. Reconciliation, rev
> rec, close: that's your product surface. I don't need a year to understand what a matching
> rule or a period close is.
>
> Second, the loop. What I want most is a short distance between seeing a problem and shipping
> the fix — small team, close to customers, deciding rather than aligning. That's what I
> couldn't get at Stripe's size, and it's what a 60-person company can actually offer.
>
> Third, the specific technical problem. The interesting thing about agents in finance isn't
> getting the model to do the work — it's that an auditor doesn't object to automation, they
> object to automation that can't show its work. Making a non-deterministic agent produce an
> auditable, reproducible, defensible trail is a genuinely hard systems problem, and it's close
> to what I've been doing: correctness, replayability, invariants. That's the problem I'd want."

Then ask one back. **What not to do:** don't say "AI is changing everything" — true of every
candidate and every company, and it signals you're shopping the sector rather than choosing
them. Don't say "well funded" out loud; it reads as looking for safety, the opposite of what a
founder wants to hear.

## L) The other founder questions

The five you originally prepped are HM questions. A CEO adds these, and you currently have
answers for none of them.

| Question | The move |
|---|---|
| **"What are you exceptionally good at? What are you bad at?"** | One real strength stated flatly, one real flat spot with what you do about it. See [§B](#three-traps). |
| **"Tell me something you believe that most engineers you've worked with disagree with."** | Founders love this. See below. |
| **"Why not start something yourself?"** | Have a real answer. "I'd rather build than sell / I want the technical problem" is fine; visible hesitation is not. If you have thought about it, say so — that reads as ambition, not disloyalty. |
| **"Where do you want to be in three years?"** | Ambition, concretely. Owning a surface of the product, or a small team. "I'll see where it goes" is a non-answer at this bar. |
| **"What would you change about our product?"** | A live *Courage over comfort* test. Use the product if you can get access, or read their reconciliation and AP pages, and arrive with one real opinion held lightly. Having no opinion is the worst outcome. |
| **"How would you build X for us?"** | He may go straight at the product. The [R2 notes](01-r2-system-design-round.md) cover the substance. |

### The contrarian question

Pick **one**, hold it with conviction, have the evidence:

- **Most production failures in financial data systems are correctness, not infrastructure —
  and almost nobody instruments for them.** The incident categorisation is your evidence. Green
  dashboards, wrong numbers.
- **Freshness is over-valued relative to recoverability.** Teams optimise the happy path and
  discover at the worst moment that they can't reconstruct state. This is S4.
- **Reconciliation is mostly a data-onboarding problem, not a matching problem.** The matching
  engine is the interesting-looking part and the smallest part of the work.

The third is strongest if you believe it, because it is an opinion about *his business* rather
than about engineering — it is his cost structure. Be ready for him to push back hard; that is
the point of the question, and folding immediately fails it.

## M) Questions to ask them

Ask three or four. With a CEO, ask about the **business**, not the engineering process.

**Product and the hard part:**
- Where does the agent hand off to a human today, and what's the plan for moving that line?
- Auditability is what you sell against incumbents — how do you make a non-deterministic
  agent's output reproducible for an auditor? Is there a replay story?
- Reconciliation at a bank vs. at DoorDash — how much of the matching logic is shared versus
  per-customer? What does onboarding a new customer cost you in engineering time?

**Business:**
- Open-source models self-hosted, or API? How does inference cost show up in gross margin, and
  does that shape the architecture?
- What breaks at the next 10x of customers — is it the product, the onboarding, or the team?
- Where is the product weakest today?

**The role and the bar:**
- What does this person need to have done by month six for you to be glad you hired them?
- Where does the team currently move slower than you'd like?
- You list *Courage over comfort* — what's a recent decision where someone took the risky path,
  and how did it land?

That last one tests whether the values are real, and asking it is itself a *Courage over
comfort* signal.

## N) Gaps to close before the round

Ordered by how much they cost you if left open.

1. **Establish the counterfactual in [§F](#f-the-spike-question).** Were you the one who called
   the model change, with the team otherwise tuning? Yes or no changes the whole lead answer.
2. **Find what sub-3-hour freshness unblocked** — business consequence, in one sentence. This
   is property 3 and it's missing from your single most important story.
3. **Numbers for S3.** The whole story is qualitative. Dig out honestly: how many incidents you
   categorised and over what window; how many checkers you shipped; how many bugs caught in
   staging that would have reached production; whether incident volume in those categories
   fell; how many teams adopted it. Approximate and honest beats precise and invented.
4. **A failure story.** You have none, and it's near-certain at lead level. It must be a real
   failure with a cost you owned — not S6, since changing your mind after a POC is a success
   story with humility in it, and interviewers read the substitution as evasion. Candidates: an
   incident your pipeline caused, a deadline you missed, an estimate you got badly wrong.
5. **Your contrarian belief** (§L), stress-tested against the obvious rebuttal.
6. **One honest weakness**, specific, with what you do about it.
7. **An opinion about Zamp's product.** Use it if you can get access; read the reconciliation
   and AP pages if you can't.
8. **The ending of S5** — did adoption clear the threshold, did phase 2 ship?
9. **Two real invariants** in finance language, one sentence each (§J).
10. **Say these out loud, timed:** the arc (§D, 75s), the spike answer (§F, 90s), why leaving
    (§G, 60s), the break (§H, 30s). These four are the ones that need to sound settled rather
    than assembled — hesitation on them is exactly what he's listening for.
